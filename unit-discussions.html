UNIT 01 · Initial post published 3 August 2026

Initial post — Industry 4.0 and Maersk

Industry 4.0 technologies, including automation, cyber-physical systems, Internet of Things devices and interconnected information systems have significantly transformed the logistics sector (Efthymiou and Ponis, 2021). They allow logistics companies to obtain real-time information, improve the visibility and monitoring of goods, and coordinate activities across warehouses, transport networks and supply chains (Chauhan et al., 2023). Industry 4.0 systems also generate and process large volumes of operational data, supporting faster decision-making and more efficient industrial operations (Raptis, Passarella and Conti, 2019). However, increased connectivity can also create vulnerabilities because a failure affecting an important information system may rapidly disrupt physical operations and interconnected supply chains. Cheung, Bell and Bhattacharjya (2021) observe that although internet-based technologies improve logistics services, their adoption also increases the sector’s exposure to cyberattacks.

A significant example is the NotPetya cyberattack experienced by A.P. Moller–Maersk on 27 June 2017. The attack disrupted information systems across the company’s transport and logistics operations, causing operational difficulties, reduced shipping volumes and disruption to customers (A.P. Moller–Maersk, 2018). During the recovery, Maersk reportedly had to rebuild approximately 4,000 servers and 45,000 computers within ten days (Greenberg, 2018). Employees also relied on improvised and partly manual processes while the company’s digital infrastructure was being restored. The scale of this reconstruction demonstrates Maersk’s dependence on globally interconnected information systems.

Maersk estimated that the attack reduced its profitability by approximately US$250–300 million through lost revenue, IT restoration costs and extraordinary operational expenses (A.P. Moller–Maersk, 2018). The impact also extended beyond the company itself. Crosignani, Macchiavelli and Silva (2023) found that the effects of NotPetya spread from directly affected organisations to their customers, producing a fourfold amplification of the initial reduction in profits. This demonstrates how the failure of a major logistics provider’s information systems can affect organisations throughout a supply chain.

The incident highlights an important limitation of Industry 4.0: systems designed primarily for integration, connectivity and efficiency may become vulnerable when the same connectivity allows failures to spread across systems and locations. Metcalf (2024) explains that Industry 5.0 initially emerged partly as a reaction to the excesses associated with Industry 4.0 technologies. From an Industry 5.0 perspective, logistics systems should therefore prioritise resilience and human involvement alongside automation and efficiency. Measures such as network segmentation, offline backups, tested recovery procedures and manual operational alternatives could reduce the effects of similar incidents. The Maersk case demonstrates that human adaptability remains essential, but it should complement resilient system design rather than compensate for its absence.

UNIT 03 · Summary published 18 August 2026

Summary — cybersecurity and recovery

In my initial post, I discussed the benefits of Industry 4.0 technologies in logistics, including improved visibility and monitoring of goods, better supply-chain coordination (Chauhan et al., 2023), and faster decision-making (Raptis, Passarella and Conti, 2019). However, I also highlighted that increased connectivity can expose logistics organisations to greater cybersecurity risks (Cheung, Bell and Bhattacharjya, 2021).

I used the NotPetya cyberattack experienced by A.P. Moller–Maersk on 27 June 2017 as an example. The incident resulted in an estimated US$250–300 million impact on Maersk's profitability (A.P. Moller–Maersk, 2018), while the effects also spread to customers and other organisations within the supply chain (Crosignani, Macchiavelli and Silva, 2023).

My colleague John-Jon expanded the discussion by arguing that although connectivity amplified the impact of NotPetya, connectivity itself was not the root cause. He highlighted the governance challenges associated with externally acquired technologies, drawing on NIST guidance, which suggests that organisations may have limited visibility into how third-party technologies are developed, integrated and introduced into their systems (Boyens et al., 2022). He also emphasised that manual alternatives alone are insufficient and should instead be supported by resilient system architecture, trusted data and rehearsed recovery procedures.

Bashair further contributed by identifying weaknesses related to third-party software, patch management and network segmentation. She also highlighted that Maersk's recovery partly depended on fortunate circumstances, including the survival of a domain controller in Ghana (Greenberg, 2018). This reinforced the importance of tested offline backups and recovery procedures. She also raised the question of whether large logistics providers should be required to demonstrate recovery capabilities in a manner similar to safety compliance.

Overall, the discussion reinforced that resilience and human adaptability should be treated as important elements of Industry 5.0 alongside automation and efficiency. It also highlighted the need for stronger governance of third-party technologies and tested recovery capabilities. Cyberattacks may not be completely preventable, but their operational and supply-chain impact can be significantly reduced through appropriate technical controls, governance and recovery planning.

UNIT 08 · Initial post published 19 September 2026

Initial post — AI writing: benefits and ethical risks

The rapid development of AI writing systems creates significant opportunities for productivity, but also risks of harmful or discriminatory outputs. Hutson (2021) explains that although systems such as GPT-3 can produce convincing human-like text, they generate language from statistical relationships rather than genuinely understanding its meaning. This can have serious consequences. Hutson (2021) reports that GPT-3 could generate hate speech and racist or sexist stereotypes and describes a test in which a healthcare chatbot gave a dangerously inappropriate response when asked about suicide. Therefore, I believe the appropriate use of AI writers depends strongly on the purpose of the writing and the consequences of an incorrect or harmful output.

For low-risk administrative work, AI writers can provide considerable benefits. Tasks such as drafting routine emails, summarising documents or correcting grammar are repetitive and can consume a considerable amount of employees’ time. Automating these activities could allow workers to concentrate on more productive tasks. There may also be accessibility benefits, particularly for people writing in a second language. Nazari, Shabbir and Setiawan (2021), for example, found that an AI-powered writing assistant improved several measures of engagement and writing self-efficacy among postgraduate English-language learners.

However, the risks increase when generated text is used in situations where outputs can affect people. LLMs can produce fluent and authoritative-sounding information even when the content is incorrect or harmful. Furthermore, models trained on large collections of human-produced text can reproduce biases contained within their training data. Abid, Farooqi and Zou (2021), for example, identified persistent anti-Muslim bias in GPT-3 and found that prompts mentioning Muslims generated violent completions considerably more frequently than prompts referring to other religious groups. Bender et al. (2021) similarly warn that large language models can reproduce discriminatory patterns present in their training data. Therefore, AI-generated content used in areas such as healthcare, law or other high-risk applications should be subject to human verification.

Creative writing presents a different challenge. Factual accuracy may be less important, while AI can support brainstorming, overcome writer’s block or suggest alternative formulations. However, extensive reliance on generated content raises questions about authorship and originality. Formosa et al. (2025) found that the degree of AI assistance influenced how people judged a writer’s authorship, creatorship and responsibility for a creative work. There is also a risk that models trained on existing writing reproduce established patterns rather than creating genuinely novel perspectives.

Overall, AI writers could be useful for low-risk administrative writing or as assistants in creative writing. On the other hand, human oversight is needed in areas where an inappropriate output could have serious consequences. Therefore, the acceptable level of AI autonomy should depend on the level of risk and uncertainty associated with an incorrect or harmful output.

UNIT 10 · Summary published 17 October 2026

Summary — a broader responsible-AI framework

The discussion reinforced my original view that the acceptable use of AI writing systems should depend on the level of risk associated with their outputs, but it also showed how this framework could be strengthened.

First, human oversight should involve more than fact-checking. Damiano highlighted that biases can originate in training data and remain present even after alignment and safety measures. In high-risk areas such as healthcare, recruitment or law, reviewers should therefore consider both factual accuracy and possible discriminatory or stereotypical outputs.

Patrick’s response also raised the question of who should perform this oversight. Weidinger et al. (2021) argue that biases vary across social and cultural contexts, meaning that affected communities may identify harms that developers or general reviewers overlook. This suggests that high-risk systems may require multidisciplinary and participatory review. I also agree that non-deployment should remain an option where acceptable levels of fairness or reliability cannot be demonstrated.

Lana’s contribution further showed that risk should be assessed in the specific deployment context rather than only according to the task. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework similarly emphasises contextual risk assessment (Tabassi, 2023). Bias benchmarks, automated red teaming and human review could therefore be combined to identify problematic behaviour before deployment.

Another important point is that even apparently low-risk uses can create wider risks. Liang et al. (2023), for example, found that AI-text detectors disproportionately misclassified writing by non-native English speakers. This shows that risk assessment should consider not only the generative model, but also surrounding tools, policies and decisions.

Overall, I would refine my original framework into a broader risk-management process. Low-risk applications may allow greater AI autonomy, while higher-risk uses require stronger testing, transparency, stakeholder oversight and monitoring. Where acceptable reliability or fairness cannot be achieved, the responsible decision may be not to deploy the system.
